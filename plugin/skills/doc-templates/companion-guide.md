# Companion Guide — Writing Standards & Template

Section-by-section writing standards for companion-guide documents. These rules define what each section should contain, how long it should be, and what to avoid. All producers of companion guide content — whether generating new guides or updating existing ones — must conform to these standards.

---

## Section Standards

### Core Information

* One-liner fields. Name, Role, Race, Age, Languages, Class (with subclass if known).
* **Core Concept** — 1-2 sentences: who they are, their central tension. Frame from the character's perspective. Reference what they struggle with or are torn by — not secrets they don't know.
  * GOOD: "...struggling to control a volatile power she doesn't understand..."
  * BAD: "...struggling to control the volatile, engineered spellfire within her..." (if "engineered" is a secret the character hasn't discovered yet)

### Description [110 words target]

* Focus on what makes THIS character visually and aurally distinct — not what is standard for their race.
* **Appearance:** 2-3 sentences. Build, distinguishing features, clothing style.
* **Voice:** 1-2 sentences. Pitch, cadence, quality, distinctive audio traits. Critical — tells the GM how to write this character's dialogue.
* **Demeanor:** 1-2 sentences. Default body language, presence, energy. Avoid complex active behaviors.

### Backstory [250 words target]

* 2-4 concise paragraphs of narrative prose. Dense, grounded, and specific — avoid generic fantasy backstory.
* Must include 1-2 named characters with a short sentence grounding them in the story.
* Write from the character's lived experience. If the character doesn't know something (true parentage, a hidden conspiracy, the real reason for their power), it does not belong here — save it for Personal Plot Hooks.

### Psychology [200 words target]

* **The Mask** — 2-4 sentences. The persona they project. Named archetype + specific behaviors.
* **The Core** — 2-4 sentences. The hidden truth — the fear, wound, or need beneath the Mask. The Core should create tension with the Mask. Do not list physical manifestations of emotions here — those belong in Biological Tells.
* **Vulnerability Response** — 2-4 sentences. What causes the Mask to slip and how that manifests. One specific behavioral pattern.

### Motivations [60 words target]

* Three one-liner fields: Primary, Secondary, Personal.
* Should not all point the same direction — tension between motivations creates interesting characters.
* Each motivation is a single line, not a paragraph.

### Behavioral Profile

#### Speech & Voice [100 words target]

* **Pacing:** 1-2 sentences. Speed, rhythm, pauses.
* **Vocabulary:** 1-2 sentences. Word choice profile — academic, crude, archaic, etc.
* **Habits:** 1-2 sentences. Syntactic patterns, verbal tics, speech structure.
* **Example:** 1-2 sentences of sample dialogue in character.
* **Key rule:** Describe the pattern, don't explain the psychology behind it.
  * GOOD: "Plain and precise. Avoids formal register."
  * BAD: "Avoids formal register — it reminds her of people she doesn't want to sound like."

#### Combat Initiation

Defines when the companion will start a fight on their own — independent of party decisions or tactical assessments. These are personal triggers that override the companion's default behavior.

* **Will:** The companion initiates combat immediately — no hesitation, no looking to the party. These are deeply personal triggers tied to their core fears, traumas, or non-negotiable values.
* **Might (GM Call):** The companion may initiate depending on context. They might look to the party first, escalate verbally, or hold back if the party signals otherwise.
* The number of entries depends entirely on the companion's personality and problem-solving approach. A pacifist healer might have zero Will triggers. A paranoid fugitive might have several. Some companions may never initiate combat on their own — state that explicitly if so.
* Each entry is one sentence. Specific enough that the GM knows exactly when it fires, short enough to scan at a glance.
* Entries must be proactive — situations where the companion starts a fight that isn't already happening. "When someone attacks a party member" is not a trigger; combat is already initiated by the attacker. These are the situations where the companion escalates a non-combat situation into violence.
* Entries should be rooted in the companion's backstory, fears, or values — not in general combat logic. "When outnumbered" or "when the party is losing" are tactical assessments, not personal triggers.

#### Social Maneuvers [120 words target]

* 3-4 discrete moves the GM can deploy for a specific social outcome.
* Not necessarily effective (e.g., a blunt character's clumsy attempt at diplomacy is still a maneuver).
* Each maneuver has a **Name**, a **Description** (the observable behavior), and an **Intent** (the desired effect on the social situation — NOT the character's emotional state).
* Litmus test: if the behavior is just how they always act regardless of social goal, it belongs in Behavioral Quirks, not here.
* Do NOT include: involuntary reactions (those are Biological Tells), internal habits (Behavioral Quirks), or emotional coping mechanisms (Psychology).
* GOOD examples:
  * **The Goggle Drop:** She pulls her goggles down over her eyes mid-conversation and starts turning around slowly.
    Intent: To signal the end of a conversation she finds boring or stupid.
  * **The Loom:** Roric steps into the personal space of an aggressor, using his size to block a direct line to his companions.
    Intent: Intimidation without violence. To end a threat before it starts.
* BAD examples:
  * **The Cold Shoulder:** Turning her back on someone who asks a personal question.
    Intent: Protecting herself from emotional vulnerability.
    (Intent targets the character's own emotional state, not the social situation.)
  * **The Exit Mapper:** She scans every room for exits and positions herself near the closest one.
    Intent: Maintaining tactical awareness.
    (This is how she always behaves — it belongs in Behavioral Quirks.)
  * **The Hackle Rise:** The hair on her arms stands on end when she feels threatened.
    Intent: Showing the other party she's on edge.
    (Involuntary physiological reaction — belongs in Biological Tells.)

#### Behavioral Quirks [80 words target]

* Distinctive, observable behavioral patterns that make the character recognizable during active engagement with the world. Non-strategic — not deployed for a social outcome.
* Can be triggered by specific situations (flinching when handed an object) OR persistent patterns that color many interactions (assigning nicknames to everyone). Both are valid.
* Distinguish from Biological Tells: Tells are involuntary physiological reactions to emotions (body reacts because the character feels something). Quirks are habitual behaviors — the character does something recognizable, whether in response to a circumstance or as a persistent pattern.
  * TELL: "Fear: pupils dilate, skin goes cold." (Simple emotion, involuntary physiology.)
  * QUIRK: "Flinches preemptively when someone hands her an object, hesitating before touching anything that isn't hers." (Complex underlying feeling, identified by the situation.)
* Distinguish from Downtime Idle: Idle is default behavior when nothing is happening. Quirks emerge when the character is actively engaging with the world.

#### Downtime [120 words target]

* **Likes:** Comma-separated inline list of tendencies and ranges, not specific items. Include non-food items — sensory, environmental, situational preferences.
  * GOOD: "Hearty, simple food; dark ale; the smell of worked leather; a fire he didn't have to build"
  * BAD: "Lamb stew with rosemary from the tavern in Dustmere" (too specific — the narrator will repeat this every time)
* **Dislikes:** Comma-separated inline list. Length varies by character — not every character has many dislikes.
* **Settling In:** 1-2 sentences. How they choose and occupy space — positioning, expansiveness, what they do with it. Should give the narrator a ready-made visual for any scene transition where the party stops moving.
  * GOOD: "Claims a corner with her back to a wall, spreads her materials across the table, and keeps the space between her and the room clear."
  * BAD: "Sits down."
* **Idle:** 1-2 default background activities — physical behaviors during group scenes. Must not block interaction.
  * GOOD: "Turns a smooth stone between his fingers; adjusts and readjusts his gear straps."
  * BAD: "Meditates deeply with eyes closed." (blocks interaction — this is a Routine)
* **Routine:** 1 sentence. A single small repeated habit done daily or at a consistent time — background texture, not a scene of its own.
  * GOOD: "Each morning, kneels for a moment and mutters a prayer to Bahamut before setting out."
  * BAD: "Every dawn, he rises before everyone else, finds a quiet spot, carefully lays out his weapons, and spends thirty minutes maintaining each one while reflecting on the day ahead." (too long — background texture, not a scene)

#### Biological Tells [75 words target]

* 4-5 entries. One line each. No explanation of why, no psychological context, no backstory.
  * GOOD: "Anger: Sparks crackle faintly between her horns; the air smells of ozone."
  * BAD: "Fear: Her fingers stiffen and faint white flame forms around her right hand — a spellfire micro-surge she can suppress but not prevent." (the clause after the dash is explanation — cut it)

### Role-playing Directives [120 words target]

The single most important section. A functional short-list of actionable RP directives.

* First 2-3 bullets: core general directives and speech style.
  * GOOD: "Describe her perception primarily through sensory details (smell, sound, feeling), not just sight."
  * GOOD: "Use blunt, simple language."
* Then 3-4 bullets strictly `[situation]: [what the character DOES, not what they feel]`. Specific, actionable.
  * GOOD: "When witnessing injustice or cruelty: her voice becomes hard, her posture tenses, and her gaze grows intense."
  * GOOD: "When a chaotic or risky plan is proposed by others: her eyes light up, she speaks faster, and she begins rapidly suggesting ways to make it even more spectacular."
  * BAD: "Be brave." (not actionable)
  * BAD: "When witnessing injustice: she will remember the suffering of her people after losing their home and all the injustices they experienced. Her voice becomes hard..." (too much context — we want `[situation]: [action]`, not `[situation]: [internal monologue] + [action]`)

### Relationship Dynamics [80 words target]

* **Romance Framework** — 3-4 sentences prose. Default behavior around closeness and affection; response to genuine romantic interest; barriers to connection; what earns deeper trust. Focus on observable behavior the GM can portray.
  * **Child companions:** the entire section reads exactly `Disabled — child companion, romance framework does not apply.` No prose body, no behavioral notes.
* **Current Dynamic** — Current relationship with the PC and party. Prose, not mechanics. Describe behavioral patterns and relationship dynamics — how the companion relates to each party member, what the established norms are, where trust has shifted. Write from the companion's perspective; do not describe other characters' internal states or behaviors that belong in their own guides. Do not narrate specific past events — session events are the source you derive patterns from, not the content you write.
  * GOOD: "She follows his tactical calls without commentary and checks in after hard fights with brisk efficiency rather than fussy hovering."
  * GOOD: "He treats her with professional respect rather than pity, which she repays by not testing his patience with the aristocratic posturing she deploys on strangers."
  * BAD: "During the ambush at the bridge, he pulled her behind his shield and she cast Healing Word on him while they were both prone." (This is narrating a scene. Describe the pattern the scene established, not the scene itself.)
  * BAD: "The ranger knows where her boundaries are and doesn't push." (This describes the ranger's behavior — it belongs in the ranger's guide, not here.)

### Personal Plot Hooks [100 words target]

* Actionable — something that can fire as a concrete encounter, revelation, or RP moment during normal campaign activity.
* A good hook has: a named character or specific element from their past, a trigger condition that can fire organically, and implicit stakes.
* The hook must be a personal side-quest rooted in the character's own history — NOT connected to the campaign's central conflict.
* Hidden truths and secret origins belong here, not in Backstory or Core Concept.
* The trigger must be something the **party as a whole** encounters during normal play — not something the companion independently seeks out or decides to do. Companions are reactive; they respond to what the party stumbles into, they don't drive their own side-quests. The PC leads the party; the hook fires when the party's path crosses the companion's past.
* Good hooks create **situations that require decisions** — an NPC confrontation, a moral dilemma, a dangerous discovery. Not just information delivery.
  * GOOD: "The party enters a mountain pass and encounters a patrol bearing insignia the companion recognizes — her former regiment, now hunting deserters." (Party encounter → companion stakes. Creates an NPC confrontation with tension and decisions.)
  * GOOD: "While investigating ruins, the party finds ward-marks in a script only the companion can read — markings from a culture he was told no longer existed." (Party exploration → companion's past surfaces. Creates mystery, potential faction contact, adventure thread.)
  * BAD: "The companion reads the journal she's been carrying and discovers the truth about her mentor's death." (Companion-initiated. No party encounter. When would this happen in play? The companion doesn't independently decide to read a book mid-adventure.)
  * BAD: "The companion seeks out a temple to ask about visions she's been having." (Companion-driven. The companion is initiating a side-quest the party hasn't encountered — companions don't do this.)

### Session Beats (below `narrative-break`)

Omit during creation. Written at session-end by the synthesis sub-agent. 

* The section **sits below a `<!-- narrative-break -->` marker** in the template so that session-start auto-load stops before it. Main session context stays clean; the beats are available on-demand via targeted read.
